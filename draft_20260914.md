**HEADLINE**  
SEC’s latest enforcement blitz and cross‑border CCP drill signal tighter scrutiny on advisory filings, AI use, and fund‑raising practices – fintech compliance teams must act now.

---

### WHAT HAPPENED  

Over the past week the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission released five separate announcements that together paint a clear picture of the regulator’s current enforcement focus.  

First, senior officials from the SEC, the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, the CFTC and the Bank of England convened on Sept. 3 for a tabletop exercise on central‑counterparty (CCP) resolution. The joint read‑out highlighted gaps in coordination between U.S. and U.K. authorities and underscored the importance of robust governance and data‑sharing protocols when a CCP faces distress.  

Second, the SEC announced four enforcement actions.  
* Ernest Ossei Boateng and two New Jersey entities were charged with running a $16 million Ponzi‑type scheme that promised high returns on “wealth‑building” products.  
* Mark D. Hanf and former COO Hoai‑Nam Chu Phan of Pacific Private Money Group were accused of a multimillion‑dollar offering fraud that misled investors about the performance and risk of private‑fund investments.  
* Thirty‑eight entities were charged for filing false Form ADV documents between 2025‑2026, deliberately portraying themselves as registered investment advisers to attract retail investors.  

Finally, the SEC’s Investor Advisory Committee will hold a public meeting on Sept. 10 to discuss the growing use of artificial‑intelligence (AI) tools in the public markets and the agency’s approach to overseeing those technologies.

---

### WHY IT MATTERS  

**Cross‑border CCP readiness** – The joint U.S.–U.K. exercise shows regulators are preparing for a scenario where a CCP fails and must be wound down quickly. Fintech firms that provide clearing, settlement or data‑aggregation services to CCPs—or that rely on CCP‑derived data for pricing or risk models—need to ensure their contingency plans align with both U.S. and U.K. expectations. Gaps in data‑flow, governance or reporting could trigger supervisory inquiries or limit market access.  

**Heightened enforcement on fraudulent fundraising** – The two Ponzi‑scheme cases and the false‑advisor filing action demonstrate that the SEC is aggressively targeting entities that use sophisticated marketing, social‑media outreach, or “wealth‑creation” narratives to lure retail investors. Fintech platforms that host or facilitate fundraising, token offerings, or advisory services are now under a brighter spotlight. Even if a platform merely provides the technology, the SEC may consider it a “facilitator” if it fails to conduct adequate due‑diligence on the issuers it lists.  

**AI in the market is moving from novelty to regulatory focus** – The upcoming Investor Advisory Committee meeting signals that the SEC will soon issue guidance—or possibly rulemaking—on how AI‑driven analytics, trade‑execution bots, or sentiment‑analysis tools can be used without violating market‑fairness rules. Fintech firms that embed AI into client‑facing dashboards, robo‑advisors, or compliance monitoring must anticipate new disclosure, testing, and model‑risk expectations.  

**Retail‑investor protection is front‑and‑center** – All three enforcement actions involved misleading statements aimed at everyday investors. The SEC’s emphasis on accurate Form ADV disclosures means that any fintech that markets itself as an adviser, or that helps advisers file, must double‑check that all material facts—fees, conflicts, performance history—are truthful and up‑to‑date. Failure to do so can lead to civil penalties and reputational damage that spreads quickly across digital channels.  

**Operational risk from regulatory coordination** – The CCP drill highlighted the need for clear lines of communication between compliance, legal, risk‑management and IT teams when dealing with cross‑jurisdictional incidents. A breakdown in any of those functions could be interpreted as a lack of “reasonable supervision,” a concept the SEC has used to hold senior officers accountable in recent enforcement actions.  

---

### WHAT TO DO  

- **Review and tighten CCP‑related data flows** – Map every data feed, API and reporting line that supports clearing or settlement functions. Verify that you have documented backup procedures, secure data‑sharing agreements with U.K. counterparts, and a clear escalation path for a CCP‑failure scenario.  

- **Enhance due‑diligence on any fundraising or advisory client** – Before onboarding a new issuer, fund, or advisory firm, run a checklist that includes: verification of registration status, review of Form ADV (or equivalent) filings, background checks on key personnel, and a risk rating for the marketing claims they intend to use on your platform.  

- **Audit AI models used in market‑facing products** – Create an inventory of all AI‑driven tools that influence pricing, trade execution, or investment recommendations. For each model, document the data sources, validation results, bias‑mitigation steps, and a plain‑language description of how the model works. Prepare to disclose this information to regulators if requested.  

- **Update disclosure and marketing review processes** – Institute a “two‑eyes” sign‑off for any client‑facing material that mentions performance, fees, or risk. Ensure that any claim can be backed by verifiable data and that all required regulatory disclosures (e.g., Form ADV sections) are current.  

- **Conduct a cross‑functional tabletop exercise** – Replicate the SEC‑Bank of England CCP scenario within your own organization. Involve compliance, legal, IT, risk, and senior management. Identify gaps in communication, data availability, and decision‑making authority. Document lessons learned and integrate them into your business‑continuity plan.  

By acting on these steps now, fintech compliance teams can stay ahead of the SEC’s enforcement trajectory, protect retail investors, and maintain smooth operations across borders and emerging technologies.
