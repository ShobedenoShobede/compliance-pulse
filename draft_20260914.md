**HEADLINE**  
SEC’s latest enforcement blitz and cross‑border CCP exercise signal tighter scrutiny of fund structures, AI use, and false adviser filings – fintech compliance teams must act now.

---

### WHAT HAPPENED  

Over the past week the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission released a flurry of announcements that together paint a clear picture of the agency’s current enforcement priorities.

1. **Cross‑border CCP resolution drill** – On Sept. 3 senior officials from the SEC, the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, the CFTC and their U.K. counterpart at the Bank of England held a tabletop exercise on how a central counterparty (CCP) would be resolved in a crisis. The meeting focused on coordination, information sharing, and the regulatory triggers that would force a CCP into liquidation or restructuring.

2. **Four major enforcement actions** –  
   * **Ponzi‑style fraud in New Jersey** – Ernest Ossei Boateng and two entities he controls (Intercontinental Wealth Network LLC and I Wealth Network LP) were charged with raising roughly $16 million from more than 200 investors through false promises of high‑return wealth‑management services.  
   * **San Francisco Bay Area private‑fund scheme** – Former CEO Mark D. Hanf and former COO Hoai‑Nam Chu Phan of Pacific Private Money Group LLC were accused of a multimillion‑dollar offering fraud that misled investors about the fund’s performance and risk profile.  
   * **False adviser filings** – The SEC charged 38 entities for submitting materially false Form ADV information between 2025 and 2026, creating the illusion that they were registered investment advisers when they were not.  
   * **Investor Advisory Committee on AI** – The SEC announced a public meeting on Sept. 10 to discuss the growing use of artificial‑intelligence tools in the public markets and the agency’s approach to overseeing those technologies.

All five releases are now on the SEC’s newsroom site and are being cited in internal briefings across the industry.

---

### WHY IT MATTERS  

**1. Enforcement is moving from “big‑ticket” cases to a broader net of everyday fintech activities.**  
The two Ponzi‑scheme charges involve relatively small, founder‑led firms that used digital marketing, social‑media outreach, and online investment portals—channels that many fintech platforms also rely on. The SEC’s willingness to bring criminal‑style charges against such operations signals that any fintech product that promises guaranteed returns, uses “high‑yield” language, or obscures risk will be examined closely. Compliance teams must treat marketing copy and investor disclosures with the same rigor as a traditional broker‑dealer would.

**2. False adviser filings are a direct threat to fintech firms that act as “registered investment advisers” or that host third‑party advisers on a marketplace.**  
The 38‑entity enforcement shows that the SEC is scrutinizing the accuracy of Form ADV data, especially the “business practices” and “client types” sections. For fintech platforms that onboard advisers, the regulator will likely probe whether the platform performed adequate due‑diligence on the adviser’s registration status, disciplinary history, and disclosed fees. A single mis‑filed ADV can bring the entire platform under investigation.

**3. The CCP resolution exercise highlights regulatory focus on systemic risk and data transparency.**  
Although the tabletop was a high‑level exercise, the SEC emphasized the need for real‑time data sharing between market participants and regulators during a CCP crisis. Fintech firms that provide clearing, settlement, or collateral‑management services must be prepared to deliver accurate, timely information to both U.S. and U.K. regulators. Failure to do so could trigger heightened supervisory scrutiny or even restrictions on cross‑border activities.

**4. AI is now on the SEC’s agenda.**  
The Investor Advisory Committee’s upcoming meeting on AI indicates that the agency will soon issue guidance—or perhaps rulemaking—on how AI models are used for market analysis, trade execution, and investor communication. Fintech companies that rely on machine‑learning algorithms for pricing, robo‑advice, or predictive analytics should anticipate new disclosure requirements and risk‑management expectations.

**5. The speed of the SEC’s announcements underscores the need for rapid internal response.**  
All five releases were published within a single week. Regulators are clearly capable of moving quickly, and the market expects firms to do the same. Compliance functions that still rely on quarterly reviews or ad‑hoc “once‑a‑year” training will find themselves outpaced.

---

### WHAT TO DO  

- **Audit all marketing and investor‑communication material** for promises of guaranteed returns, “high‑yield” language, or undisclosed risks. Replace any such language with clear, balanced disclosures and retain a record of the review.  

- **Implement a formal Form ADV verification process** for any adviser you host or partner with. This should include: (a) checking the adviser’s registration status in the Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) system; (b) confirming that all material disclosures (fees, disciplinary history, client types) are up‑to‑date; and (c) maintaining a secure audit trail of the verification.  

- **Strengthen data‑sharing protocols for clearing and settlement activities.** Map the data fields the SEC and Bank of England expect in a CCP crisis (e.g., margin calls, open positions, collateral valuations) and test your ability to export that data within 24 hours. Document the process and run a tabletop drill internally.  

- **Review AI model governance.** Create a checklist that covers: model purpose, data sources, validation results, bias testing, and a clear “human‑in‑the‑loop” policy for any decision that could affect investors. Prepare a concise summary for senior management that can be shared with regulators if requested.  

- **Set up a rapid‑response monitoring team.** Designate a small group (compliance, legal, and product) to scan SEC releases daily, assess relevance, and issue internal alerts within 24 hours. Use a shared repository for the alerts so that the entire compliance function can see the latest regulatory developments at a glance.  

- **Conduct a “red‑flag” training session within the next two weeks.** Focus on the hallmarks of the recent Ponzi‑type schemes—unrealistic performance claims, pressure to invest quickly, and reliance on personal networks. Ensure all client‑facing staff can recognize and report these signals.  

- **Update your third‑party onboarding questionnaire** to include explicit questions about false filing histories, AI usage, and cross‑border clearing capabilities. Require supporting documentation for each claim and schedule a follow‑up review before any new partnership goes live.
