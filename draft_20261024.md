**HEADLINE**  
SEC Rolls Out 2027 Exam Focus, Cross‑Trading Rule Change, and New International Cooperation – What Fintech Compliance Teams Must Track Now  

---

### WHAT HAPPENED  

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) released its Division of Examinations priorities for fiscal year 2027. The agency said it will concentrate on three high‑risk areas: (1) the use of digital assets and related custody arrangements, (2) the oversight of private‑fund formations that target non‑accredited investors, and (3) the adequacy of anti‑money‑laundering (AML) programs at broker‑dealers and investment advisers. The report also flags “emerging technology” and “cyber‑risk” as cross‑cutting themes that examiners will probe across all firm types.  

In a separate move, the SEC signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with France’s Autorité des Marchés Financiers (AMF). The agreement creates a formal channel for sharing information on investment advisers and investment‑service providers that operate in both the United States and France. The MOU is intended to speed up supervisory reviews, coordinate enforcement actions, and reduce duplicated effort when a firm is subject to both regulators.  

The SEC’s Small Business Capital Formation Advisory Committee (SBAC) announced a meeting on October 27, 2026, to continue its work on modernizing the initial public offering (IPO) process and to explore changes to Regulation Crowdfunding. The committee will hear from market participants about simplifying the registration pathway for emerging companies and about possible adjustments to the $5 million annual fundraising cap and disclosure requirements for crowdfunding portals.  

At the same time, the SEC proposed amendments to the Investment Company Act’s “cross‑trading” rule. Currently, a registered fund may trade securities with its affiliates only under limited circumstances. The proposal would broaden the list of securities that can be traded in this way, provided the fund can demonstrate that the transaction is at fair market value and does not disadvantage shareholders.  

Finally, the SEC moved for a final judgment by consent against Stephen Kenneth Leech II, former co‑chief investment officer of Western Asset Management. The judgment resolves a “cherry‑picking” case in which Leech allegedly steered certain securities to the firm’s private‑client accounts while keeping better‑performing assets for the firm’s own funds, violating fiduciary duties and securities laws.  

---

### WHY IT MATTERS  

**Exam focus on digital assets, private funds, and AML** – Fintech firms that offer crypto‑custody, tokenized securities, or automated investment advice must expect deeper scrutiny. Examiners will look for robust controls around wallet security, clear segregation of client assets, and documented AML risk assessments. Even companies that do not directly hold digital assets can be affected if they partner with custodians or provide “wrap‑around” services. The emphasis on private‑fund formations means any fintech platform that enables non‑accredited investors to join a fund must be able to prove that it is meeting the SEC’s suitability and disclosure standards.  

**Cross‑border cooperation with the AMF** – For fintechs that serve both U.S. and French clients, the new MOU means regulators can share data more quickly. A compliance lapse in France could trigger a U.S. investigation, and vice‑versa. Firms should therefore align their policies with the stricter of the two regimes, especially around client onboarding, record‑keeping, and reporting of suspicious activity. The MOU also signals that the SEC is willing to pursue joint enforcement, raising the stakes for any gaps in cross‑border supervision.  

**Potential IPO and crowdfunding reforms** – The SBAC’s upcoming discussion could lead to lower thresholds for public listings and higher fundraising caps for crowdfunding portals. Fintech platforms that host equity crowdfunding or provide IPO‑related services should monitor the meeting’s outcomes closely. A change in the $5 million cap, for example, could expand the addressable market but would also require updates to investor‑qualification checks and ongoing disclosure obligations.  

**Expanded cross‑trading permissions** – If the SEC adopts the proposed rule, registered funds will be able to trade a broader set of securities with affiliates, provided they can prove fairness. Fintechs that develop fund‑management software, trade‑execution engines, or data‑analytics tools for registered funds need to incorporate new validation checks, pricing transparency logs, and audit trails. Failure to capture these details could expose the fund and its service provider to enforcement action for “unfair” transactions.  

**Enforcement precedent from the Western Asset case** – The judgment against the former co‑CIO underscores that “cherry‑picking” – giving preferential treatment to certain accounts – remains a serious breach. Fintechs that automate portfolio allocation or provide “best‑execution” guarantees must ensure that any internal routing logic cannot be used to favor one client segment over another. Documentation of the decision‑making process, regular independent reviews, and clear conflict‑of‑interest policies are now even more critical.  

Overall, the five updates point to a tightening regulatory environment for fintechs that touch securities, digital assets, or cross‑border services. The SEC is signaling that it will look harder at technology‑driven models, will cooperate internationally, and is ready to adjust long‑standing rules to keep pace with market innovation. Compliance teams that act now can avoid costly examinations and stay ahead of potential rule changes.
